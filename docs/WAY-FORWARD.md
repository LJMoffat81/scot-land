# Way forward

Review of 2 October 2026. The editor returned a verdict, kept the answers used below, and struck none. This review did not fail verification. Findings are only those kept answers. Nothing here is said in the voice of a named person, and nobody named is claimed to endorse this site. Where a seat says its text is a reading, it stays a reading. This review does not pick one tax by averaging the seats. It does not treat half of GDP, the configured £90 billion pool, or the research tax basket as figures it has newly proved.

## What the site should be

The site should be a plain page for the public and for politicians. It should say, in ordinary words and under separate labels, what each opened source actually proposes, what that proposal does not replace, and what is still missing before a pound can be a rate. A figure on the map stays the signed-off open-data research estimate, not an official rates bill, not a national total made by adding squares, and not the Scottish budget. The page should not be a land-ownership atlas, and it should not be one blended charge.

The kept answers do not describe the same charge. Each of the following has to be sayable on its own. None of them is a licence to merge the others.

Wightman, *A Land Value Tax for Scotland* (October 2010), requires a plain account of a local levy on the unimproved value of land alone, not on buildings, drainage or services, offered in place of council tax and the uniform business rate. That report says the levy cannot be calculated until Scotland has the extent and location of all land use, the extent and location of every ownership parcel, the highest and best use of each parcel, and a value for every parcel, and that councils would run it from a land-use database and the Land Register. The same report says the Scottish Parliament had no power to levy a new national tax and could only set the local framework, so a national rate of 1p in the pound was a cut in central grant, not a new national tax. Source: https://andywightman.scot/wp-content/docs/LVTREPORT.pdf

Ogilvie's essay requires the page to say what may be claimed and what may not, without depending on a name. Each person has by nature a right to possess and cultivate an equal share of the soil. No one can draw from that right a title to more than an equal share. What stays with the improver is the accessory or improved value, including on land beyond that share. What the community may claim is only the original value and the still-improvable value of the surplus beyond an equal share. Inside the equal share, original, improved and contingent value stay with the holder, and a holding no larger than that share is exempt. Until that original-value fund is exhausted, the essay says the expenses of the state ought to be carried by the land-tax, and that taxes on other property, on consumption, and burdens on trade, manufacture and arts, come after it. That is the argument. It is not a Scottish budget and not a bill for a map square. This is a reading of the essay as printed in M. Beer (ed.), *The Pioneers of Land Reform* (1920), pp. 35–38 and 41–47. The opened text is https://www.cooperative-individualism.org/ogilvie-william_an-essay-on-the-right-of-property-in-land.pdf

Sandilands' 13 May 2020 note requires the national replacement he published, not a local rates notice: rent as the product of society and nature, argued to be about half of GDP, or about half of national income, rather than the textbook figure of about 5% or less, because taxes on earned incomes squeeze the rent of land, and the aim is to replace taxes on earned incomes and trade until ground rent could finance a modern state, whose spending the note puts at about 40% of GDP. That half is his judgement about rent after the replacement. It is not a figure this review has proved, not a line read off the accounts, and not a pound on one place. Source: https://slrg.scot/blog/rent-is-half-of-gdp/

Pickard's 23 August 2018 note requires a charge on productive value once speculative price is taken out, not on the price paid today. Prime farms are given as about five times productive value, £8,000 an acre against £1,600, so the worked 1% levy is £16 an acre, not £80, with the same warning for residential and urban sites. Chargeable rental value is only the surplus above locations that can provide no more than subsistence. Collecting that surplus is the published alternative to taxing earnings. The note is not a finished assessment of one household, the current Scottish budget, or an independent state. This is a reading of the note, not a claim that its author endorses the site. Source: https://slrg.scot/blog/calculating-agrlvt-charges-when-council-tax-and-income-tax-are-replaced/

The SLRG pages require the published case in plain words. Annual Ground Rent is the market rent of each site, collected to fund public services, so taxes on employment and enterprise can be reduced or scrapped. It is one charge for owning land, including estates, farms and the ground under houses and businesses, and it does not take the money from wages or trade. The methodology already uses that definition. The signed-off disclaimer already says a map figure is an open-data research estimate, not an official rates bill. On that account the charge is the page, not a land-ownership atlas and not a tax demand. That requirement is this seat's. It does not settle the other seats. Source: https://slrg.scot/blog/what-is-agr

The Land Commission requires a plain account of the foundation the March 2026 advice says is still missing, not a finished charge. If government wants tax to deliver land policy, raise revenue or unlock productivity, all land should be on the valuation roll or an equivalent database, with better integrated information on use and ownership, and residential values still rest on 1991 prices. The building blocks named are trusted land data and mapping, work on valuation methods, the legal framework, clear land-use priorities, the wider fiscal effects, and the cost of administration. A pound on a map must not look like a rate that advice has set. This is a reading of the published advice, not a Commission endorsement of this site. Source: https://www.landcommission.gov.scot/downloads/SLC-Tax-Advice-FINAL.pdf

Fraser of Allander requires the site to state the Scottish budget the Institute published, and to say that those pages do not test a charge on land. *Scotland's Budget Report 2026-27* (9 January 2026) says the Scottish Government will use a one-off boost to offset an underlying resource deficit of around half a billion pounds in 2026-27, that June capital plans of £8.1 billion are well above the £7.1 billion available even after borrowing, and that the tax rises required to plug the longer funding gap look undeliverable, largely because Scotland has fewer very high earners, so it is hard to see a response that does not cut spending. Source: https://fraserofallander.org/publications/scotlands-budget-report-2026-27/

Common Weal requires a council-run tax on the present market value of buildings and land together, paid to local authorities for local services. The opened paper says a separate land tax may be unnecessary, and it rejects both a land value tax and an annual ground rent. Its illustration is a flat 0.63% chosen only to match Council Tax revenue, not as an optimum and not as a rate the Scottish Government should impose. On the paper's 2022 land values that extension raises about £443 million a year under current use, which the key points round to around £450 million, of which Highland Council is given about £97 million. Income tax, a little under £13.5 billion, is not the tax being replaced. The power it uses is the Scotland Act clause on local taxes to fund local authority expenditure. A new national tax is described as open to a UK veto. The stated purpose is also to cut speculative land prices and concentrated ownership, not to stand in for taxes on wages. Source: https://www.commonweal.scot/s/Taxing-Land.pdf

REVIVE requires a labeled campaign proposal, not a finished substitute for taxes on wages and production. The 2025 manifesto's Land Tax (Scotland) Bill would tax the value of non-residential landholdings, defined as land that is not a house or garden, with discounts and exemptions such as food-producing land and land with high public-interest grades. Local authorities should implement it, and some proceeds should be available for communities. The primary goal is to make large holdings less attractive so communities and cooperatives can buy them. Money for the local area is secondary. That is a campaign ask. It is not a claim that REVIVE endorses this site, and it is not a charge the manifesto says replaces the Scottish budget. Source: https://revive.scot/wp-content/uploads/REVIVE-2025-Manifesto-for-Real-Land-Reform.pdf

Community Land Scotland requires a plain account of what the opened briefings say community ownership needs: more of Scotland in community hands, and taxation used to deliver that change in who owns land. The November 2025 key asks call for 10% of Scotland in community ownership by the end of the next Parliament, a plan to expand community ownership and reduce concentrated private ownership, a Public Interest Test on all large-scale ownership, and fair taxation to deliver land-reform outcomes, plus a Scottish Land Fund of £25 million with a ring-fence for buying land and a Community Wealth Fund seeded by payments from renewables, storage and transmission. The opened text does not define that fair taxation as a charge which replaces taxes on wages and production. This is a reading of the key asks, not an endorsement of this site. Source: https://www.communitylandscotland.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/Community-Land-Scotland-Manifesto-Key-Asks-2025-26.pdf

The landowner case has to stand beside the charge, in plain language, and be labeled as Scottish Land & Estates' case rather than as an endorsement. The statement of 10 December 2020 says a land value tax would affect every corner of Scotland, urban and rural, not only estates or large landowners; that it could discourage investment by housing developers, forestry businesses and farmers and could affect communities' aspirations; that a change of this magnitude should be judged by its economic effect, not only by land-reform objectives; and that many of the tax functions in the report they were answering are reserved. The statement of 27 October 2025 states a separate condition: further land reform must be proportionate, evidence-based and workable, and on that account they treat forced lotting and a property transfer test as an unworkable interference in the land market. A page that shows only a charge replacing taxes on wages, aimed at large holdings, is not the case politicians will meet. Source: https://www.scottishlandandestates.co.uk/news/sle-statement-land-and-property-taxation-report

## What it should stop

Stop showing a pound on a map square as a tax bill, and stop implying that those pounds add up to a national rent, to a report's totals, or to the Scottish budget. Wightman's 2010 totals are an approximate construct from published averages: land value of the order of £120 billion, a 3.16p levy raising about £3.8 billion in place of council tax and the uniform business rate, and £12 billion a year if the levy took the whole economic rent at a 10% discount rate. The report says the household comparison uses Scotland-wide averages, that land may be as low as 10% of property value and as high as 60% in Edinburgh, and that without the location of land use it is impossible to set a rate for a given yield. Adding squares does not recreate those totals.

Stop implying that vacant or derelict land is where the money is. In that 2010 illustration, urban vacant land is 1.37% of the yield and derelict land is zero-rated. Stop redrawing Who Owns Scotland holdings as if they were this tax map. The March 2025 note records concentration, not parcel values: 421 landowners own half of privately owned rural land, compared with 440 in 2012. Source: https://andywightman.scot/2025/03/who-owns-scotlands-2024/

Stop showing the equal share as the cash line in this repo. `docs/methodology.md` and `data/config/agr.yaml` define an equal annual claim as an estimated Scotland rent pool divided by population, compare a map square with that claim, and mix the formula with Paine and Unitism. The essay does not define the share as a national rent pool, does not add fields up into that pool, and does not make a map figure a tax bill. Stop the shorter lineage line that the community claim is simply land rent and that improvements are merely private. Inside an equal share the holder keeps the original value as well as the improved value. Only the surplus yields original and contingent value to the community, and the improved value of that surplus stays with the improver. Stop offering the essay's English illustration, 100 acres sold for £1,500, money at 5 per cent, contingent value £500, original value £200 or £300, and improved value £700 or £800, as a Scottish rate. Stop treating the progressive Agrarian's ceiling of forty acres as a measure for a map square. The footnote says that standard ought to vary with the country, from sixty acres to twenty in Europe.

Stop adding the map to the national pool, and stop reading a square as a tax bill. The config's rule is that the national surplus is the stored pool multiplied by the scenario rate, not the sum of the map's residual cells, and that those cells are not calibrated to total the £90 billion labelled National rent pool (Sandilands). The same lines call the residual an open-data research approximation, not a live tax bill. They also say non-domestic, vacant and natural-resource rents are not on the roll, so the map must not be shown as the whole rent that pool stands for. The file stores a 0.50 share and £90 billion with no GDP total or year beside them. That store must not be shown as a rent checked against this year's Scottish GDP, and this review does not prove it.

Stop letting the on-screen pounds a year, the one-person pays or receives line, and the pills labelled Income tax, Council tax and rates, and Listed taxes be read as the bill that replaces those taxes for a household. Pickard's 2018 note says current site values are not a good guide to the liability. The only charge it calculates is the farm example, £16 an acre of productive value, not £80 of today's price. It calculates no house, states no urban percentage, and adds no sites up into a national total.

Stop printing the research basket as if those pounds were the taxes this charge replaces, and stop setting a square's pounds-a-year beside a national above-the-list total. The config marks the basket as a demo placeholder, not an official forecast, and says the national surplus is not the sum of map squares. Stop the pills that shorten the signed-off label Replace all listed taxes (neutral) to Listed taxes. The SLRG homepage's income-and-trade list is income tax, National Insurance, VAT, corporation tax and excise. The signed-off scenarios are only the three placeholder lines. Stop leading with public, Crown and vacant hectares. That is where the screen buries the charge. This review does not prove the basket.

Stop leading with a land-only pound a year, and stop pills that read as if that pound replaces income tax, council tax and rates, or a basket of listed taxes. The Land Commission's October 2025 valuation report says Scotland's system values property including buildings, not land by itself, and that domestic values are still 1991 bands rather than a current figure for each holding. It says there is no formal revaluation of homes, no systematic national data collection, and no national single database. The countries it studied do not share one rule for valuing land separately from buildings, and setting a tax rate is a separate political step from the valuation. A map square priced as land alone is not Scotland's rateable value and should not be shown as a bill. Source: https://www.landcommission.gov.scot/downloads/68f737cad6890_Approaches-to-land-valuation-in-the-tax-system.pdf

Stop showing the configured lines of £11.5 billion of income tax, £2.8 billion of Council Tax and £2.8 billion of non-domestic rates as the Institute's figures, and stop any control that reads as if a land charge replaces all taxes on wages and production. The config calls those pounds research placeholders, not an official forecast. Table 1.4 of the 2026-27 report states a non-domestic rates distributable amount of £3,549 million inside resource funding. It does not state an income-tax yield or a Council Tax yield, and a pound on a map square is not that table. Source: https://fraserofallander.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/2026-27-Scotlands-Budget-Report.pdf

Stop showing a charge on the land alone, including a pounds-a-year line for the land and not the building, as if it were Common Weal's proposal, and stop pills that offer that figure as a stand-in for income tax, for council tax and rates, or for a listed tax basket. The paper says an annual ground rent set so that income tax and VAT could be abolished might balance a book but would drop progressive income tax and taxes used to change behaviour. Stop implying one map square is that tax bill, or that squares can be added up to the paper's total. That total is the paper's own sum, about £443 million, and councils, not a national screen, are to set the rate.

Stop implying that a charge on the land under a house, or on food-producing land, is the tax REVIVE describes, and stop implying that a pound on a map square is that tax bill or that squares add up to a national rent pool. The manifesto's bill leaves out houses and gardens and gives food-producing land as an example exemption. Its only rate is an illustration: taxing the value of land at, say, 0.63% could more than cover Highland Council's deficit, with the workings pointed to another paper. It sets no binding rate and no national yield. Stop showing one ownership count. The manifesto says 421 people, organisations and corporations own half of Scotland's private land and names no study. The homepage says 421 landowners own half of Scotland's private rural land, and the same page also displays 408 for private rural land. Those are campaign wordings, not one official statistic. A value layer is also not the separate commitment to a public map of who owns what land and how it is used.

Stop the pills that set a map square against income tax, council tax and rates, or the listed-tax basket, and stop implying that a rent collected while the land stays in private hands is the reform Community Land Scotland describes. The 9 December 2025 article says the Land Reform Bill still does not intervene structurally in the land market and does not change the financial and tax incentives that encourage accumulation. It says reforms to taxation and inheritance, and a shift in farm and forestry subsidies toward smaller locally owned businesses, could cut land prices and limit accumulation. It says community ownership cannot do this alone, and that most land should be owned and managed by communities, farmers, local businesses or the public sector. A pound on a square is not that outcome, not a tax bill, and not a total made by adding squares.

Stop showing the charge as if it fell only on large estates, and stop implying that a research figure for one map square is the tax bill that replaces taxes on wages and production or replaces non-domestic rates. The 10 December 2020 statement says the proposals would affect the entire countryside, not simply estates, and it states no yield and names no wage tax the charge would replace. The paper announced on 17 September 2026 proposes to keep non-domestic rates as a property-based tax, adjusted with allowances, so that local authority income is protected. The objections opened on 27 October 2025 are to lotting, a transfer test, prior notification and land-management plans. They are not a national total made by adding map squares.

## The disagreements that decide the path

These are not one tax. Wightman 2010 is a local levy on unimproved land alone, in place of council tax and the uniform business rate. Common Weal rejects a land-only land value tax and an annual ground rent, and illustrates a locally set tax on the present market value of buildings and land together. Pickard charges productive rental value after speculative price is removed. Sandilands and the SLRG homepage argue for replacing taxes on earned incomes and trade with ground rent. Averaging those seats would invent a tax none of them published.

Wightman in September 2026 argues for an up-to-date valuation of the whole property in £10,000 bands, not a levy on land alone. That disagrees with the 2010 land-only levy and with a land-only annual ground rent. The 2010 alternative budget only illustrates a 3p cut in the basic rate of income tax, £120 million for community councils and £12 million for a land fund, by lifting the same approximate valuation to a 4.392p levy said to raise £5.28 billion. That sketch is not the 2026 proposal, not the Scottish budget, and not an independence settlement. The September 2026 post does not replace taxes on wages and does not fund an independent Scotland. Source: https://andywightman.scot/2026/09/parliament-can-now-scrap-the-council-tax/

On power, the opened pages do not agree what this Parliament can do. Wightman 2010 said the Scottish Parliament could not levy a new national tax, so a 1p rate was a cut in central grant. Common Weal says a new national tax is open to a UK veto and uses the local-tax clause so councils fund local services. Sandilands ties a Scottish switch to full fiscal devolution. The Land Commission says the reserved and devolved mix already limits the Scottish Government. Scottish Land & Estates says many of the 2020 tax functions are reserved. The SLRG homepage states a UK-wide case and does not say which taxes this Parliament can replace.

On what is replaced, do not average the yields. Wightman 2010 illustrates council tax and the uniform business rate, about £3.8 billion at 3.16p, and a separate 4.392p sketch of £5.28 billion that only illustrates a 3p income-tax cut. The SLRG homepage lists income tax, National Insurance, VAT, corporation tax and excise, and says the pool is half of UK gross national income. Common Weal does not replace income tax of a little under £13.5 billion and illustrates about £443 million a year for councils. Pickard publishes £16 an acre on a farm, not a household or a budget. Fraser of Allander does not test a land charge. The essay's remark that proprietors of stock in trade, manufacture and arts are the proprietors of one-half the national income is an illustration of who would escape other taxes once a land-tax carried the expenses of the state. It is not a measurement that Scottish ground rent is half of national income, and it is not Sandilands' half.

Ogilvie's equal share is a right to possess and cultivate an equal part of the soil. The community claim is the original and still-improvable value only of the surplus, and a holding no larger than the share is exempt. The repo's equal-share line is a cash claim, a rent pool divided by population, mixed with Paine and Unitism. The essay does not support that formula.

Wightman 2010 puts urban vacant land at 1.37% of the illustrated yield and derelict land at zero, so vacant land is not where that illustration's money is. The Land Commission's missing valuation roll is a condition before tax can be used, not a yield from vacant hectares. ScotLand Futures says there is not currently sufficient data on land use, value and ownership for Scotland to use existing or new tax powers in a transformative way. Source: https://www.landcommission.gov.scot/next-steps-for-land-reform/4-delivery-co-ordinated-use-of-policy-tax-and-fiscal-levers

Ownership counts are not one statistic. Wightman's March 2025 note says 421 landowners own half of privately owned rural land, against 440 in 2012. REVIVE's manifesto says 421 people, organisations and corporations own half of Scotland's private land and names no study. The REVIVE homepage also displays 408 for private rural land. Common Weal's Our Land key points say 432 families own half of private rural land. Do not average them, and do not treat 421 or 408 as an official statistic. The manifesto names no study for 421, and the homepage's 408 line links a report this seat did not open.

The site share is not one figure. The SLRG homepage says a typical UK home is 60% site value and that house prices and rents fall by at least half. The signed-off config sets `use_slrg_for_display` to false. Pickard's 2018 note gives no urban percentage. The config's 0.70 urban discount is marked SLRG-tuned. Wightman 2010 says land may be 10% of property value, or 60% in Edinburgh, on Scotland-wide averages. Common Weal refuses to tax land apart from the building. The methodology page still says the display default is a 60% site share, while that display flag is false. These are not one site share, and the homepage's halving of prices is not a result this screen may state while the flag is false.

No opened page funds this year's Scottish budget, or an independent Scotland, from this map. Fraser states a 2026-27 resource gap of about half a billion and capital plans of £8.1 billion against £7.1 billion available, and does not test a land charge. Its GERS guide is a backwards look at the current settlement and says little about the long-term finances of an independent Scotland if the economy and society changed. It does not publish separate pound totals for Council Tax or non-domestic rates, and it does not test a land charge. Common Weal's *Scotland's Fiscal Future* does not mention land, ground rent, or a property tax. It treats a Scottish currency on day one as the future-neutral choice, and it says sterlingisation is what would force tight budgets. Sandilands publishes no Scottish rent in pounds. The Land Commission sets no land-value rate. The wealth share it cites, over half of UK wealth held in land and property, is not a Scottish tax yield.

Scottish Land & Estates says a land value tax would hit every corner, urban and rural, including farms, forestry and housing, not only estates, and should be judged by economic effect. REVIVE's bill excludes houses and gardens, gives food-producing land as an example exemption, and aims first at making large holdings cheaper for communities. Community Land Scotland pairs fair taxation with a Public Interest Test and community ownership. Its April 2024 paper explores a tax complementary to council tax, a flat-rate percentage of land and property values, and cites Common Weal for up to £650 million extra from a 0.63% tax on buildings and around £450 million more if that rate is extended to land. It does not turn those figures into this year's budget or a sum of map squares. That is not a national ground rent in place of taxes on wages. Source: https://www.communitylandscotland.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/Fiscal-measures-briefing-April-2024.pdf

Pickard says current site values are not a good guide: prime farms at about five times productive value make a 1% levy £16 an acre, not £80 of today's price. Common Weal taxes the present market value of building and land together in order to hit speculative price, and rejects a charge on productive or economic value. Do not blend £16 an acre with 0.63% of market value. Common Weal's further illustrations also wait: £497 million depends on poorer land becoming as valuable as commercial forestry, and a Danish-style 3.4% said to yield £2.4 billion is described as unlikely and not sustainable on current farm income.

Fraser's Table 1.4 prints a non-domestic rates distributable amount of £3,549 million and states no income-tax or Council Tax yield. Forecast devolved revenues of £23,044 million in that table are a bundle, not an income-tax yield. The config's £11.5 billion, £2.8 billion and £2.8 billion are marked research placeholders. Wightman's 2010 uniform business rate sits inside a £3.8 billion pair from that year's illustration. None of these is a land-charge bill, and none is the sum of map squares.
