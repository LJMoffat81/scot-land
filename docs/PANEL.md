# Panel

Sitting of 2 October 2026. The editor returned a verdict. This sitting did not fail verification. What is solid is only the kept claims. Struck claims are gaps, not findings. Nothing here is said in the voice of a named person, and nobody listed below is claimed to endorse this site.

## The site job

Scotland Open Land is a simple place where people and politicians can see a charge on land as a replacement for taxes on wages and production, for the current Scottish budget and for a future independent Scotland. This sitting does not treat that replacement as already shown. The base of the charge, the taxes it would stand in for, and what an independent Scotland could fund from ground rent are disputed below. A pound on a map square is an open-data research estimate, not a rates bill, not half of UK gross national income, and not the Scottish budget.

## What is solid

The editor kept the claims below. Each one has its seat and its source. They are not merged into one valuation or one tax bill.

### Wightman

- Who Owns Scotland 2024 (December 2025, v.2) says private entities own 83% of rural Scotland (6,393,504 ha). Of privately owned rural land, 421 landowners own 50% (440 on the revised 2012 figure) and 2,588 own 70% (3,161 in 2012). Public bodies own 875,543 ha (11.4%) and community bodies 220,458 ha (2.9%). The January 2025 update had named the owner of 74.4% of rural Scotland, plus a further 6% where the holding was known but the owner was not. Urban Scotland (179,811 ha) is outside that rural analysis. Seat: Wightman. Source: Andy Wightman, Who Owns Scotland 2024 (December 2025, v.2). https://andywightman.scot/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/WOS_2024.pdf
- A Land Value Tax for Scotland (2010) defines the charge as a levy on the market value of land alone, excluding buildings and other improvements. In the worked examples a 10% discount rate turns capital value into rental value. Vacant sites and agricultural land are included and valued at optimum permitted use. The report says the yield cannot be calculated without the extent and location of all land use and every ownership parcel, highest and best use, and a value for every parcel, and that Scotland then had no such database. It also says the Parliament had no power to levy a new national tax and could only set the local framework, so a 1p national element was to be taken as a cut in central grant. Seat: Wightman. Source: Andy Wightman, A Land Value Tax for Scotland (October 2010). https://andywightman.scot/wp-content/docs/LVTREPORT.pdf
- The same report's national totals are an approximate construct from published averages: land value of the order of £120 billion (the table sums to £120,309,900,300), a 3.16p levy raising about £3.8 billion in place of council tax and the uniform business rate, and about £12 billion a year if the levy took 100% of economic rent at a 10% discount rate. On Scotland-wide averages it says bands A–D (75% of properties) would pay less and bands F–H more, that land may be as low as 10% of property value and as high as 60% in Edinburgh, that urban vacant land is 1.37% of the illustrated yield, and that derelict land is zero-rated. Those figures are the report's illustration. They are not a bill for a map square, and adding squares does not recreate them. Seat: Wightman. Source: Andy Wightman, A Land Value Tax for Scotland (October 2010), Figures 8–10. https://andywightman.scot/wp-content/docs/LVTREPORT.pdf
- In the September 2026 post, domestic council tax for the existing bands is still based on 1991 values. The reform argued there, and in the February 2024 post it links, is an up-to-date valuation of the whole property in £10,000 bands, which could be put into the Council Tax (Scotland) Bill. That is a tax on the whole property, not a land-only assessment. The opened 2026 post does not state proportionate multipliers. Seat: Wightman. Source: Andy Wightman, Parliament can now scrap the Council Tax (September 2026), linking the February 2024 post. https://andywightman.scot/2026/09/parliament-can-now-scrap-the-council-tax/

### Ogilvie

- From common occupancy, each person has by nature a right to possess and cultivate an equal share of the soil, described as little different from the free use of the open air and running water. No one can draw from that right a title to more than an equal share of the soil of his country. That share is a birthright: entering society submits it to rules made for the general good, but does not silently give it up. Seat: Ogilvie. Source: William Ogilvie, An Essay on the Right of Property in Land, Part I, sections 1–4 (ditext transcription headed 1781). https://ditext.com/ogilvie/1.html
- What stays with the improver is the value labour adds. Of land beyond an equal share, the owner still has the accessory or improved value, but the original value of the soil and the contingent or improvable value remain with the community and may be claimed by the legislature. The essay treats that original value as a public fund a land-tax may draw until it is exhausted, and it exempts holdings no larger than the natural share. That is not a Scottish rate and not a charge on one map square. Seat: Ogilvie. Source: William Ogilvie, An Essay on the Right of Property in Land, Part I, sections 9 and 12–14 (ditext transcription headed 1781). https://ditext.com/ogilvie/1.html
- In the essay's progressive Agrarian sketch, a citizen of twenty-one who holds no land may claim an allotment not exceeding forty acres, paying a reserved rent to the landlord and compensation to the former occupier for improvements. Someone already occupying that share is not to be put off without compensation for labour and improvements. Forty acres is that sketch's ceiling, not a figure for this map. Seat: Ogilvie. Source: William Ogilvie, An Essay on the Right of Property in Land, Part II, section 71, article 1, and section 72 (ditext transcription headed 1781). https://ditext.com/ogilvie/10.html
- This repo's methodology and agr.yaml define an equal annual claim as an estimated Scotland rent pool divided by population, then compare a map square with that claim, and they mix that formula with Paine and Unitism. The essay does not define the share as a national rent pool, does not add fields up into that pool, and does not make a map figure a tax bill. Where the two differ, the essay governs. Seat: Ogilvie. Source: scotland-open-land docs/methodology.md, Equal-share illustration, and data/config/agr.yaml equal_share, read against the essay, Part I. https://ditext.com/ogilvie/1.html

### Sandilands

- The published note says rent is about half of GDP, or about half of national income, rather than the textbook figure of about 5% or less. The justification given is ATCOR: taxes on earned incomes, including income from man-made capital, squeeze rent on land as land, so replacing those taxes would raise annual ground rent until, on that account, it could finance a modern state. That is his judgement, not a line in the national accounts and not a cash total checked against this year's Scottish GDP. Seat: Sandilands. Source: Roger Sandilands, Rent is half of GDP, not 5%, SLRG, 13 May 2020. https://slrg.scot/blog/rent-is-half-of-gdp/
- The 2015 paper, as copied in this repo, does not publish a Scottish rent in pounds. For the UK in 2011 it gives GDP at market prices of £1,537 billion and gross operating surplus of £436 billion (28.4%), against an official rent on land of £417 million, described as mainly agricultural land and onshore mineral royalties and as excluding offshore oil. Most land and natural-resource rent is argued to sit hidden inside profits, building income and owner-occupation. Classical Land in that paper includes spectrum, offshore oil, harbours, landing slots and roads. Seat: Sandilands. Source: Roger J. Sandilands, Rewards from Eliminating Deadweight Taxes (repo text of the 2015 SLRG paper), section 2. C:/Projects/scotland-open-land/docs/sources/sandilands-hidden-potential.txt
- The taxes that paper argues rent could replace are deadweight taxes on wages, on the profits of productive enterprise, and on spending, including tariffs and quotas. For Scotland the switch is tied to full fiscal devolution, from taxing earned incomes to collecting unearned land rents. As a comparison, UK personal income tax is given as 26.9% of revenue in 2012–13, plus 16% from national insurance. The only Scottish fiscal scale calculated there is need, not a measured rent stock: GERS for 2008/09–2012/13 puts revenues, including a geographical share of North Sea oil, at 36.5% of Scottish GVA on average (range 34.2% to 38.6%), with expenditure averaging 42.7% of GVA. Over 1964–65 to 2013–14, UK tax revenues are given as averaging 34.3% of GDP. Seat: Sandilands. Source: Roger J. Sandilands, Rewards from Eliminating Deadweight Taxes (repo text), sections 1.1 and 5. C:/Projects/scotland-open-land/docs/sources/sandilands-hidden-potential.txt

Its own rule is that national surplus is that pool times the scenario rate, not the sum of map residual cells, and that those cells are not calibrated to sum to the £90 billion pool. Non-domestic, vacant and natural-resource rents are not on the roll. Place pounds are an open-data residual approximation, not a live tax bill. The £90 billion figure was not checked against this year's GDP. Seat: Sandilands. Source: scotland-open-land data/config/agr.yaml, macro block and integrity lines. ### Pickard

## Disagreements

These stand. They are not averaged into one valuation, one ownership count, one rate, or one tax bill.

1. Wightman's opened 2010 report levies the market value of land alone, excluding buildings, at permitted use, and rentalises capital at the discount rate used in the examples. Pickard's opened 2018 note then cuts today's price back to productive value: fivefold on prime farms, with the same warning for urban sites but no urban percentage. Common Weal's opened policy prefers a flat percentage of the current market price of buildings and land, and says a separate land-value tax may be unnecessary. Ogilvie's essay leaves the improved value with the improver and limits the community claim to the original and contingent value of land beyond an equal share of the soil. These are different bases. They are not averaged into one valuation.
2. Ogilvie's equal share is a person's slice of the soil, not a wage and not a cash dividend. The SLRG homepage says everyone, including former land speculators, would receive a fair share of the nation's rent. This repo divides a configured national rent pool by population and calls that Ogilvie's right mixed with Paine and Unitism. Where the essay and the repo differ, the essay governs. A cash dividend and a share of the soil are both kept, and they are not the same claim.
3. Sandilands' 2020 note, and the SLRG homepage, say rent is about half of national income and could finance the state after taxes on wages and production are removed. His own opened GERS arithmetic is different: Scottish revenues averaged 36.5% of GVA and spending 42.7%, and the official UK rent-on-land line he quotes is £417 million. Fraser's opened pages do not show a present Scottish rent of half of GDP. Half of GDP is kept only as an ATCOR judgement about rent after those taxes are removed, not as rent already collected and not as today's Scottish budget.
4. What would be replaced is disputed. The SLRG homepage says income and trade taxes would be phased out because the pool exceeds all current tax revenue. Wightman's 2010 report illustrated a local levy in place of council tax and business rates and said the Parliament could not levy a new national tax. His 2026 post argues for an up-to-date tax on the whole property, not land alone. Common Weal says devolution blocks a national land tax and aims its proposal at Council Tax and local services. Scottish Land & Estates says many of the 2020 report's tax functions are reserved, and that capital gains tax, inheritance tax and developer contributions are already land value taxation. Those three arise on a sale, a death or a planning consent, so they are not an annual charge and do not answer a proposal to replace taxes on wages and production.
5. Wightman's 2010 report does publish an approximate national illustration, and a tax-reform reading can cite that illustration. The Land Commission, in the pages that opened, sets no rate and says Scotland does not yet have the data on use, value and ownership to use tax powers in a transformative way. Citing the 2010 construct is allowed. Calling it a current tax bill, or the sum of this map's squares, is not. Refusing every national figure and authorising a rate are both rejected.
6. The concentration counts are not one statistic. Wightman's December 2025 report says 421 landowners own 50% of privately owned rural land, and that the revised 2012 figure is 440. He records that an earlier edition's 432 was revised. Common Weal's Our Land page still says 432 families own 50% of private rural land. Revive's manifesto says 421 people, organisations and corporations own half of Scotland's private land and names no study. Revive's homepage says 421 landowners own half of private rural land, and the same page also displays a line that 408 landowners now own that half. 408, 421 and 432 are not averaged.
7. The purpose of a charge is disputed. Revive's bill covers non-residential land, exempts food-producing land and high public-interest grades, and aims first at making concentrated ownership cheaper for communities. Community Land Scotland treats taxation as a way to diversify who holds land and to cut prices, and its December 2025 article wants most land owned by communities, farmers, local businesses or the public sector. SLRG's charge includes the ground under houses and businesses and is offered so taxes on labour and trade can be reduced, with the rent shared even if the land stays in private hands. A buy-out programme and a rent on land that remains private are different instruments.
8. Scottish Land & Estates' opened 2020 statement says a land value tax would affect every part of Scotland, could discourage housing, forestry and farm investment, and should be judged by recovery from the economic crisis rather than by land-reform objectives alone. Jobs and activity at estate scale, on that statement, are their warning. They do not show that those outcomes would be lost under an annual charge, and no opened page of theirs states what such a charge would raise or which taxes on wages it could replace. Later Scottish Land & Estates pages were not opened, so this is not treated as their latest wording.
9. On an independent Scotland, Fraser says GERS is a backwards look at the current settlement and not a forecast of a different constitution. The opened 2025-26 balance is -£25.3 billion, -10.9% of GDP, against -4.2% for the UK. Common Weal says sterlingisation, not the absence of a land charge, is what would force tight budgets, and that a Scottish currency on day one is the future-neutral choice. Sandilands ties a Scotland switch from taxes on earned incomes to land rent to full fiscal devolution. None of the opened independence pages funds the state from ground rent. GERS is not kept as proof that a land charge either can or cannot replace that budget.
10. The urban haircut is disputed inside the sources. Wightman rentalises market value minus depreciated replacement cost and stops there. Pickard's article states the farm adjustment and does not state an urban percentage. agr.yaml's 0.70 factor is marked SLRG-tuned, not a published Pickard figure. methodology.md also still says, in its integrity notes, that the urban path is largely a council house-price average times a site share, and that the display default is the 60% SLRG split, while agr.yaml sets use_slrg_for_display to false and describes a residual of price minus depreciated rebuild. Those descriptions are not averaged into one engine.
11. Fraser's budget summary says the income-tax rises that would close the Scottish funding gap look undeliverable because Scotland has fewer very high earners, and that the balance is hard to see without slower spending growth. A land-value reading treats that pressure as a reason to replace taxes on work. The opened Fraser pages do not test a land charge, so the pressure is kept and the replacement is not.
12. The screen and the signed-off config do not say what the SLRG homepage says. The pill reads Replace all taxes.
